# Topic 2 · Sign-language classification that generalises

**Goal.** Train a classifier that recognises fingerspelled letters of the American manual alphabet from a single image,
and **prove that it works on data from a different source than the training set**: another dataset, or photos of at
least five different people taken by you. Report in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy, explain the gap,
and show which remedies close it and by how much.

Back to the [project overview](../README.md)

**Extra packages** (see [the README](../README.md#general-requirements-all-topics)): `uv add mediapipe grad-cam` (both
optional: MediaPipe for the hand crop, `grad-cam` only to check your own Grad-CAM). Check that a `mediapipe` wheel
exists for your Python version before you plan around it; the skin-colour crop is the fallback.

## Why it matters

Fingerspelling classifiers reach 99 % on the Kaggle datasets in an afternoon and fail the moment a different person
signs in front of a different wall. The public datasets contain one signer, one room and thousands of near-duplicate
video frames, so a random train/test split measures memorisation, and a better model does not change that. Detecting
this situation, measuring it honestly and fixing it is a skill that transfers to every applied machine-learning project.
It also has a human side: assistive tools for Deaf and hard-of-hearing users are only useful if they work for everyone,
not for the one person who recorded the dataset.

## Recap: distribution shift and shortcuts

A classifier is trained on samples from $p_{\text{train}}(x, y)$ and evaluated on $p_{\text{test}}(x, y)$. When the
test images come from the same video as the training images (same person, same background, same camera), the two
distributions are nearly identical and the test accuracy says little about understanding. When the signer, the
background, the lighting or the camera change, $p(x)$ shifts while the thing that determines the label, the hand shape,
stays the same. A model that learned **shortcuts** (the wall colour, the sleeve, where the hand sits in the frame)
collapses; a model that learned hand shape survives.

Two test sets, then:

- **In-distribution (ID)**: held out from the training source, as cleanly as you can (see the split notes below).
- **Out-of-distribution (OOD)**: a different source. Another dataset, or your own photos.

The **gap** $\text{acc}_{\text{ID}} - \text{acc}_{\text{OOD}}$ is your main object of study. Expect it to be large at
first (ID above 99 %, OOD anywhere between 20 % and 70 %). Your job is to explain it and to shrink it.

## Datasets

| ASL Alphabet | 87 000 train (3 000 per class) + 29 official test images | 29: A–Z, space, delete, nothing | 200×200 RGB JPEG | about 1 GB | GPL-2.0 | https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/grassknoted/asl-alphabet |
| ASL Alphabet Test | about 870 (30 per class; a different signer, varied backgrounds) | 29, same as above | RGB JPEG | about 30 MB | see page | https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/danrasband/asl-alphabet-test |
| ASL Fingerspelling A (Pugeault & Bowden) | more than 65 000 | 24 letters | RGB + depth, 5 signers, hands already cropped | several GB (download only the RGB part you need) | research use, see page | https://empslocal.ex.ac.uk/people/staff/np331/index.php?section=FingerSpellingDataset |

Properties that decide your design:

- **Sign Language MNIST** was generated by cropping and augmenting a small number of original photos. Train and test
  are near-duplicates of each other; 99.9 % test accuracy is expected and means little.
- **ASL Alphabet** consists of video frames of one person in one room; the 29 official test images are from the same
  session. A random split leaks. The classes `del`, `space` and `nothing` exist nowhere else.
- **ASL Alphabet Test** has a different signer and varied backgrounds, which makes it the cheapest OOD-test set. With
  30 images per class the per-class numbers are noisy: report $n$ and the interval.
- **Pugeault & Bowden** has five signers, so a **leave-one-signer-out** split is a genuine OOD evaluation inside a single
  dataset. The images are already cropped to the hand, which makes a cross-dataset comparison with full-frame datasets
  unfair unless you crop those too.
- The **common label space** across all sources is the 24 static letters A–I and K–Y. Map every dataset onto these 24
  and drop the rest for cross-source evaluation.

### The hard requirement: an OOD evaluation

You must evaluate on data from a different source than you trained on. Two ways to satisfy it:

1. **Another dataset.** For example: train on ASL Alphabet, test on Pugeault & Bowden (crop your training images to the
   hand first, or accept the unfairness and discuss it) and on ASL Alphabet Test; or train on Sign Language MNIST, test on
   ASL Alphabet converted to 28×28 grayscale with the same preprocessing function.
2. **Your own photos.** At least **five different people** (not only you), several backgrounds, several lighting
   conditions, left and right hands welcome. A suggested minimum that fits in an afternoon: 24 letters × 5 people × 3
   shots, about 360 photos. Ask every person for consent, frame the hand so that faces are not in the photo (or blur
   them), and keep the photos out of the public fork if the people prefer that; the evaluation code and the resulting
   numbers must be in the fork either way. Label 50 of the photos yourself, blind (file names hidden), before you run
   any model; if a person gets 60 %, the photos are the problem, not the model, and that number goes into the data
   card.

Whichever you choose: **split the OOD data into OOD-val and OOD-test** (for your own photos, two people for choosing
remedies, three people touched once at the end). If you tune on the whole OOD set it stops being out-of-distribution.
Report both ID and OOD accuracy for every variant, with the number of OOD images.

## Suggested baseline and steps

1. **Look at the data.** A grid of 20 random images per class per source. Class counts. What varies (hand shape) and
   what does not (background, skin tone, hand side, distance, lighting)? Write it down; it is your first hypothesis
   about which shortcuts the model will take. Run the duplicate check of the README's data card (md5 per file, then
   8×8 thumbnails for near-duplicates); for Sign Language MNIST, count how many test images have a near-duplicate in
   train.
2. **Common label space and loaders.** A `Dataset` per source that maps its labels onto the 24 shared letters and
   applies **one shared preprocessing function** (resize to 64×64 or 96×96 for your own CNN; 224×224 and ImageNet
   normalisation for a pretrained backbone). Verify the mapping by looking at images: Sign Language MNIST labels run
   0–25 for A–Z with 9 (J) and 25 (Z) absent; `ImageFolder` numbers ASL Alphabet folders alphabetically (A–Z, `del`,
   `nothing`, `space`), so index 9 there *is* J.
3. **ID split without leakage.** For ASL Alphabet do not shuffle: frames are sequential, so hold out a contiguous block
   (for example the last 20 % of file names per class) to reduce near-duplicates. For Pugeault & Bowden, hold out a
   signer. Say in the report what you did and why.
4. **The dumbest baseline.** A k-nearest-neighbour or logistic-regression classifier on raw 32×32 pixels
   (scikit-learn), trained on a random split of ASL Alphabet. It will score close to 99 % ID. That number is the leak
   made visible in one line; keep it as the first row of your table and compare it to the same model on the block
   split and on OOD.
5. **Baseline.** A small CNN (three conv blocks + a linear head, the CNN you built in week 7) at 64×64, about 10
   epochs, Adam 1e-3. Before the real run, do the sanity checks from the README with this topic's expected numbers:
   cross-entropy at init about ln 24 ≈ 3.18, eight images to 100 % in a few hundred steps, a watch batch of eight
   OOD-val images logged every epoch. Report ID accuracy and OOD accuracy. This gap is your starting point.
6. **Remedies.** Try at least two, one at a time, same training budget:
   - **strong augmentation**: `RandomResizedCrop`, `ColorJitter`, `RandomGrayscale`, `RandomErasing`,
     `TrivialAugmentWide`, small rotations;
   - **background replacement**: separate the hand crudely (skin-colour threshold, or the hand region from MediaPipe)
     and paste it onto random backgrounds (random crops of unrelated photos);
   - **pretrained backbone**: ResNet-18 or MobileNetV3 fine-tuned as in week 8;
   - **hand cropping** at train *and* test time with MediaPipe Hand Landmarker: crop the hand bounding box with a
     margin. This removes most of the background shortcut in one step;
   - **landmark classifier**: the 21 hand landmarks (x, y, z), normalised for position and scale, fed into a small MLP.
     Almost background-invariant, tiny, fast; but MediaPipe finds no hand in 28×28 images and fails on some crops;
   - **mixing sources** in training (never the OOD-test source), and **signer-disjoint** splits where signers are known.
7. **Metrics.** Accuracy (ID and OOD), macro-F1, per-class accuracy, a confusion matrix on OOD. Expect confusions among
   M / N / T / S / A / E (closed fists) and among U / V / R / K. Report $n$ for every OOD number and a 95 % Wilson
   confidence interval (with $n = 360$ the half-width is about 5 percentage points).
8. **Error analysis.** Your own Grad-CAM (below) on ID and OOD images: where does the baseline look? A grid of at least
   16 misclassified OOD images with predicted and true labels.

## From scratch: Grad-CAM

This is the piece you write yourself (README requirement 6). One forward hook on the last conv block keeps its
activations $A \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times h \times w}$; one backward hook (`register_full_backward_hook`, or
`torch.autograd.grad` of the target-class logit with respect to the retained activations) gives the gradient
$\partial y_c / \partial A$. The weight of channel $k$ is the spatial mean of its gradient; the map is
$\mathrm{ReLU}\big(\sum_k \alpha_k A_k\big)$, upsampled to the input size and normalised to $[0, 1]$. About twenty
lines. Check it against `pytorch-grad-cam` on one image (max absolute difference after normalisation, or the
correlation between the two maps), show both maps side by side once, then use yours. Every Grad-CAM figure in the
report comes from your function.

## Mandatory analyses

- **Table**: variant | ID accuracy | OOD accuracy (with $n$) | gap, for the baseline, each remedy and the best
  combination. Same test images for every row; the baseline and the best remedy with mean ± std over three seeds
  (README requirement 5).
- **Confusion matrix** on OOD-test for the best model, and a paragraph on the confused pairs and why they are confused
  (hand shape similarity, orientation, thumb position).
- **At least 16 misclassified OOD images** with predicted and true labels.
- **Per-person or per-source OOD accuracy**: does the model fail for everybody, or for one person / background / skin
  tone / hand side? That question is the point of the project.
- **Evidence for the shortcut hypothesis**: your Grad-CAM, or a "background only" experiment (mask out the hand; if accuracy
  stays high, the model uses the background), or accuracy on your own photos taken against the training dataset's wall
  colour vs a different one.
- **A data card** for your own photos: number of people, devices, backgrounds, lighting, hand sides, how consent was
  obtained, how faces were excluded.
- **Fair comparisons**: same budget and identical OOD-test images for every row; the preprocessing function applied to
  OOD images is byte-for-byte the one applied to training images.

## Common pitfalls

The pitfalls shared by all topics are in [the README](../README.md#pitfalls-everybody-falls-into); these are specific
to this topic.

- **Random split on video frames** is near-duplicate leakage. Split by block, by signer or by session.
- **Tuning on the OOD set.** Keep an OOD-test split that you evaluate once.
- **Label mapping errors** between datasets (the J problem above). Verify with images, not with index arithmetic.
- **Preprocessing mismatch** between training and OOD data (grayscale vs RGB, hand-crop vs full frame, resolution,
  normalisation). The OOD drop must come from the world, not from your pipeline.
- **Tiny OOD samples** give noisy per-class numbers. Report $n$ and confidence intervals; do not read a 3 % difference
  as a result when the interval is ± 5 %.
- **Detector failures.** Count how often MediaPipe finds no hand, decide what the pipeline does then (fall back to the
  full frame? reject?), and report that rate separately from the accuracy.
- **Left vs right hand.** A horizontal flip turns a right-hand sign into a left-hand sign; it is a valid augmentation,
  but if all your training data are one hand and your OOD photos the other, that alone can explain a large gap.
- **Class imbalance in your own photos** and in the OOD dataset; use macro-F1 next to accuracy.
- **Overclaiming.** Five friends in a dorm room is not "in the wild". State the limits of your OOD set.
- **Consent and privacy.** Photograph only people who agreed, keep faces out, do not publish what they did not agree to.

## Stretch goals

- **Real-time webcam demo** (OpenCV) with the MediaPipe crop and your classifier; report frames per second and what
  breaks when you move to a window with backlight.
- **Dynamic letters J and Z** from short clips (a stack of frames, or a sequence of landmarks and a small GRU).
- **Few-shot personalisation**: fine-tune on five shots per letter of a new signer. How much does the OOD accuracy for
  that signer improve, and does the ID accuracy suffer?
- **Test-time adaptation**: recompute BatchNorm statistics on a batch of OOD images. When does it help and when does it
  hurt?
- **Landmarks vs pixels**: a full comparison of the landmark MLP and the image CNN on ID, OOD and speed.
